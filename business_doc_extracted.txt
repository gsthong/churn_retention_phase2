[189] (normal): Their goal is to uncover the friction-maybe the customer’s new staff doesn't know how to use the software. The AE’s "save" involves offering three hours of free VIP training to get them re-engaged.

[193] (normal): The Value-Add Play (For Low-Value Accounts):
[194] (normal): Because it costs too much to give these users discounts, operations simply drops them into a re-engagement email sequence highlighting new features or popular content they are missing out on.
[195] (normal): ⇒ The Intervention Matrix (Business Logic)
[196] (normal): Action: The scores hit the rules engine. Customers are segmented based on their risk level multiplied by their financial value.
[197] (normal): Output: A targeted list of accounts eligible for intervention, automatically routed to the right channel.
[198] (normal): Execution & Holdout Separation (The Action)
[199] (normal): Action: Before executing the save-plays, the system deliberately holds back a random 10% of the target group (the control group). The remaining 90% receive the automated interventions.
[200] (normal): Output: High-value customers might trigger a CRM alert for an account manager to call. Mid-value customers get an automated 15% discount email. Low-value customers receive a standard feature-update newsletter.
[201] (normal): 3. The Post-Mortem & Rule Adjustment (Governance) Operations is about continuous improvement. At the end of the quarter, the VP of Customer Success and the CFO sit down to review the playbooks.
[202] (normal): Did the VIP training actually save the enterprise clients? If yes, make it standard procedure.
[203] (normal): Did the 15% discount cost us more than the revenue we saved? If yes, the ops team rewrites the playbook for next quarter to offer a 10% discount instead.
[204] (normal): ⇒ Reconciliation & Performance Audit (The Proof)
[205] (normal): Action: 30 to 60 days later, the system compares the retention rate of the intervention group against the holdout group.
[206] (normal): Output: A performance dashboard that proves incremental revenue saved. If the intervention group retained at 85% and the holdout group retained at 70%, the business process just proved it actively saved 15% of those accounts.

[343] (Title): Human psychology
[344] (Heading 2): The Endowment Effect: People assign higher value to things they already own. A customer who has customized a SaaS dashboard or accumulated points on a credit card feels a sense of ownership. Acquisition requires convincing someone to buy a promise; retention just requires reminding them of the value they already own. → Personalizing offers is the way to retain customers (Possibility of a multi-agent AI model to analyse and give specific offers that match consumers’ experience and effort) (Rationale for multi-agent AI for data analysis and offer creation)
[345] (Heading 2): Cognitive Friction & Trust: To acquire a customer, a brand must build trust from zero. A retained customer already trusts the payment gateway, knows the interface, and understands the product. By deploying a timely, personalized offer, you eliminate the friction of switching. → It is easier to retain old than to acquire new (Rationale for coming up with this business)
[346] (Heading 2): The Fogg Behavior Model (B=MAP)
[347] (normal): ​The Psychology: A customer will only execute a behavior (deciding to stay) if Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt occur at the exact same time. It is a statement that all three must be present at once. 
[348] (normal): Motivation: how much you want to act
[349] (normal): Motivation is your desire to do the behavior at the moment of the prompt. Fogg groups it into three core motivators, each with two sides: pleasure and pain, hope and fear, acceptance and rejection.
[350] (normal): The catch is that motivation is unreliable. It rises and falls across the day and across your life. You feel ready to meditate on a calm Sunday and resentful about it on a chaotic Tuesday. Building a habit on a peak of motivation is like building a house on a wave.
[351] (normal): Here is how that plays out. You decide to start flossing. The night you watch a documentary about gum disease, your motivation spikes and you floss. Three nights later the fear has faded, motivation drops, and you skip it. Nothing about you changed except the height of the wave.
[352] (normal): Because motivation swings, Fogg argues you should lean on it as little as possible. The smarter move is to make the behavior so easy that you barely need motivation at all.
[353] (normal): Ability: how easy the behavior is to do
[354] (normal): Ability is how simple the behavior is to perform right now. Fogg breaks simplicity into six factors: time, money, physical effort, mental effort, whether it clashes with social norms, and whether it breaks your routine. A behavior feels hard if it is costly on any one of them, and your scarcest resource sets the ceiling.
[355] (normal): The key reframe is that you raise your ability not by getting stronger but by making the task smaller. Reading 30 pages is hard. Reading one page is easy. An hour at the gym is hard. Two push-ups by your bed is easy.
[356] (normal): This is the heart of Fogg's "tiny" approach. Shrink the behavior until it is almost laughably small, and ability climbs high enough that even low motivation clears the bar. You can always do more once you have started, but the starting line has to be tiny.
[357] (normal): Prompt: the cue that triggers action
[358] (normal): A prompt is the cue that tells you to do the behavior now. Without one, nothing happens, no matter how motivated or able you are. Fogg is blunt about this: every behavior needs a prompt.
[359] (normal): Three kinds:
[360] (normal):  A facilitator suits high motivation but low ability, like a one-tap button that strips out friction. 
[361] (normal): A signal suits high motivation and high ability, where you just need a reminder, like a sticky note. 
[362] (normal): A spark suits high ability but low motivation, pairing the cue with a reason to act.
[363] (normal): In Tiny Habits, Fogg adds a practical way to build your own prompt: anchor the new behavior to a routine you already do. When you say "after I pour my morning coffee, I will write one sentence," the coffee is your cue. You borrow the reliability of an old habit to trigger a new one, which is exactly how habit stacking works.
[364] (normal): The most common reason a habit dies is a missing or weak prompt. People set a goal but never decide on the exact cue that fires it. The fix is to make the prompt concrete and tie it to something that already happens every day.
[365] (normal): ​Why Our Business Model Works in the Real World:
[366] (normal): Existing predictive models fail commercially because they only deliver a Prompt (a dashboard alert). They do not solve the other two pillars. Our entire business model is built to execute the complete B=MAP equation:
[367] (normal): ​Motivation: The Multi-Agent AI dynamically calculates the exact offer (discount, feature unlock, apology) that will maximize that specific user's desire to stay, rather than guessing.
[368] (normal): ​Prompt: The system acts proactively, reaching out the moment the data signals churn, rather than waiting for the user.
